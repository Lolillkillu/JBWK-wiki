Plastics & Polymers

Thermoplastic materials used in keycaps and mechanical switch housings

Terminology Note
From a materials science standpoint, a polymer is a pure macromolecular substance — long chains of repeating monomer units (e.g., pure polyamide) — while a plastic is a formulated composition comprising a polymer matrix blended with additives (fillers, stabilisers, pigments, reinforcing fibres) ready for moulding. This page groups materials accordingly.

Glass transition temperature (Tg) marks the onset of segmental chain mobility: below Tg the polymer is glassy and rigid; above it, the amorphous regions become rubbery. In semi-crystalline polymers, crystalline domains remain load-bearing above Tg, so the usable temperature range extends towards the melting point (Tm) — this is why PBT (Tg ≈ 55 °C, Tm ≈ 225 °C) retains rigidity at temperatures that would soften an amorphous polymer with the same Tg. Amorphous polymers (ABS, PC) have no Tm and soften progressively above Tg.

Young's modulus (E) quantifies stiffness in the glassy state; it drops by 2–3 orders of magnitude above Tg for amorphous polymers, while the crystalline fraction in semi-crystalline polymers preserves a higher residual modulus. Internal damping (tan δ) — the ratio of dissipated to stored energy per vibration cycle — directly governs acoustic decay: high tan δ (e.g., ABS ~0.03, nylon ~0.03) yields muted, rapidly attenuated sound; low tan δ (e.g., POM ~0.005, PBT ~0.007) permits sustained resonance and a brighter, harder acoustic signature. Coefficient of friction (μ) depends on surface chemistry, crystallinity, and counterface; POM's exceptionally low μ (~0.1–0.2 against steel) makes it the universal choice for switch stems.

Polymers (Pure Chemical Base)

  • ABS Acrylonitrile-butadiene-styrene is a terpolymer in which the continuous SAN (styrene-acrylonitrile) matrix (Tg ≈ 105 °C) provides rigidity and chemical resistance, while the dispersed polybutadiene rubber phase (Tg ≈ −80 °C) provides impact toughness. The rubber domains act as stress concentrators that initiate crazing rather than brittle fracture, giving ABS its characteristic ductile failure mode. Young's modulus (~2.3 GPa) is moderate — lower than PBT — and the two-phase morphology gives ABS a relatively high internal damping (tan δ ~0.03 at room temperature), causing vibrations to dissipate quickly. Acoustically, this produces a muted, lower-pitched sound often described as "thock." The softer SAN surface is susceptible to shine from sebum and friction wear, and the relatively low Tg limits continuous service temperature to ~85 °C.
  • PBT Polybutylene terephthalate is a semi-crystalline polyester (Tg ≈ 55 °C, Tm ≈ 225 °C) with strong dipole interactions between carbonyl groups on adjacent chains. The high crystallinity (~30–40 %) provides excellent creep resistance and dimensional stability well above Tg, since the crystalline domains remain load-bearing up to Tm. Young's modulus (~2.7 GPa) is higher than ABS, and the low damping (tan δ ~0.007) allows vibrations to propagate efficiently with little attenuation — producing a higher-pitched, harder, more resonant sound often described as "clack." PBT is the most common keycap material because it resists surface shine: its higher hardness and crystallinity slow the abrasive wear that creates gloss on ABS. Moisture absorption is very low (~0.2 % at equilibrium), so properties are stable across humidity ranges.
  • PA6 / PA66 (Nylon) Polyamides are semi-crystalline polymers whose amide groups (—CO—NH—) form strong interchain hydrogen bonds, providing high strength and toughness. PA6 (Tg ≈ 50 °C dry, Tm ≈ 220 °C) and PA66 (Tg ≈ 70 °C dry, Tm ≈ 260 °C) differ in the number of backbone carbons between amide groups. The hydrogen bonds make both grades hygroscopic — equilibrium moisture content at 50 % RH reaches ~2.5 % for PA6 and ~2.0 % for PA66 — and absorbed water acts as a plasticiser, lowering Tg by 30–50 °C and reducing Young's modulus from ~2.5–2.9 GPa (dry) to ~1.0–1.5 GPa (conditioned). This moisture sensitivity means that switch feel and acoustics can shift subtly with ambient humidity. The hydrogen-bond network also dissipates vibrational energy efficiently (tan δ ~0.03), giving nylon its characteristically deep, muted acoustic signature.
  • POM (Acetal / Delrin) Polyoxymethylene is a highly crystalline polymer (~75–85 % crystallinity) with smooth, linear —CH₂—O— repeat units. The absence of hydrogen-bonding groups results in exceptionally low surface friction (μ ≈ 0.1–0.2 against steel) — the lowest of any unfilled engineering thermoplastic — making POM the universal choice for switch stems, where smooth linear travel and minimal stick-slip are essential. Young's modulus (~3.1 GPa) is the highest among common switch polymers, and the very low Tg (~−75 °C) means the polymer is well above its glass transition at all service temperatures, remaining tough rather than brittle; crystallinity provides the stiffness, not the glassy state. Internal damping is very low (tan δ ~0.005), so POM stems transmit vibrations with minimal loss, contributing a sharp, highly resonant character to the switch sound. Dry POM-on-POM sliding generates high-frequency friction noise, so lubrication is standard practice.
  • PC (Polycarbonate) Polycarbonate is an amorphous polymer (Tg ≈ 147 °C, no Tm) whose bulky carbonate groups and rigid bisphenol-A segments prevent crystallisation. The amorphous structure gives PC outstanding impact strength — it yields rather than shatters, with elongation at break exceeding 100 % — and excellent optical clarity. Young's modulus (~2.4 GPa) is similar to PBT, but PC is considerably less brittle. The high Tg provides thermal stability well above any switch operating temperature. The moderate damping (tan δ ~0.008) and absence of crystallite boundaries allow a very clear, high-pitched, ringing resonance. This is why "spring ping" is particularly audible inside PC switch housings — the housing efficiently transmits the spring's high-frequency oscillation rather than absorbing it.

Technical Plastics & Composites

  • M2 (Modified Polypropylene) M2 is a mineral-filled polypropylene compound. Unfilled PP has a low Young's modulus (~1.5 GPa) and poor creep resistance, making it unsuitable for precision mouldings on its own. The addition of talc or other mineral fillers (typically 20–40 % by weight) raises the modulus to ~2.5 GPa and improves flexural rigidity, impact resistance, and mould shrinkage control. PP's semi-crystalline structure (Tg ≈ −10 °C, Tm ≈ 165 °C) is retained. The mineral filler particles alter the acoustic impedance and introduce scattering interfaces, reducing the coherence of transmitted sound relative to unfilled PP. The resulting acoustic signature is often described as "papery" or slightly resonant — brighter than nylon but lacking the hard clarity of PBT or PC. The lower density (~1.2 g/cm³ with filler) also contributes to a lighter, less authoritative bottom-out feel.
  • Glass-filled PP Polypropylene reinforced with short glass fibres (typically 20–40 % by weight). The fibres carry a disproportionate share of the mechanical load, raising Young's modulus to ~4–6 GPa — roughly three times that of unfilled PP — and dramatically improving creep resistance and dimensional stability under spring pressure. The glass fibre–matrix interface introduces extensive acoustic scattering: incident sound waves are reflected and refracted at each fibre, reducing sharp resonant peaks. The result is a more controlled, less "ringy" sound than pure PC or POM, but brighter and harder than nylon. Glass-filled PP is dimensionally very stable, making it suitable for switch housings where tight tolerances must be maintained over millions of cycles.
  • POK (Polyketone) Aliphatic polyketone is a semi-crystalline terpolymer of carbon monoxide, ethylene, and a small proportion of propylene (Tg ≈ 15 °C, Tm ≈ 220 °C). The carbonyl (C=O) groups provide interchain polarity and mechanical strength comparable to nylon, but without amide hydrogen bonding — so POK absorbs very little moisture (<0.5 % at equilibrium vs. ~2.5 % for PA6). Young's modulus (~1.5 GPa) is lower than PBT or POM, giving POK a somewhat softer, more compliant feel. It exhibits outstanding fatigue resistance and a low coefficient of friction, though not as low as POM. Acoustically, POK occupies a middle ground between nylon and POM: deeper and warmer than POM due to moderate damping (tan δ ~0.02), but without the extreme muted character of moisture-conditioned nylon.
  • LY (Living Yarn) LY is a proprietary composite developed by JWK/Huano for switch housings. Its exact composition is not publicly disclosed, but it is widely believed to be a polyamide-based blend with copolymer modifiers and internal lubricants. The material is optimised for low friction against POM stems — eliminating the scratchy feel that can arise from PA-on-POM sliding pairs — while maintaining sufficient housing stiffness to produce a clean bottom-out sound. Acoustically, LY is known for the "long pole" sound profile: a distinct, sharp bottom-out impact ("clack") without the hollow resonance or friction noise characteristic of some other housing materials. Its damping characteristics appear to be tuned to suppress mid-frequency housing resonance while preserving the transient click of stem impact.
